1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY & PROBLEM STATEMENT
Modern deep learning and machine learning-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) achieve high detection
accuracy but operate as black boxes, with internal decision logic that is completely opaque to users. This
opacity poses a severe challenge in Security Operations Centres (SOCs): analysts struggle to trust automated
alerts, incident triage is slowed down, and automated response mechanisms remain highly risky to deploy. A
single false positive or misclassified benign flow subjected to automatic containment (such as a port block or
subnet quarantine) can disrupt critical business operations. Conversely, requiring manual human verification for
every alert leads to severe triage bottlenecks.
To bridge this operational gap, the authors propose XAI-IDR (Explainable AI-driven Intrusion Detection and
Response), an integrated framework that couples a high-performance hybrid detection engine with
model-agnostic explainability modules (SHAP, LIME) and a policy-driven automated response layer. This
design ensures that security analysts and downstream automation layers can act upon interpretable,
confidence-scored alerts, enabling safe automation while retaining human-in-the-loop controls for ambiguous
scenarios.
2. CORE RESEARCH QUESTIONS
The researchers designed and evaluated the XAI-IDR framework to address four key questions:
l
Detection vs. Explainability Trade-off: Can a hybrid detection engine (deep learning combined with tree-based
ensembles) match or exceed the accuracy of standalone black-box models while remaining more amenable to
explanation?
l
Real-Time Explanation Latency: Can model-agnostic explainability methods (such as SHAP and LIME) be
integrated into a live network pipeline to provide per-alert justifications without degrading real-time throughput?
l
Safe Automation Gating: Can explanation outputs be leveraged to safely gate automated response actions,
using 'explanation stability' and 'prediction confidence' as dual triggers for automatic containment while escalating
ambiguous cases?
l
Explainability Robustness: How resilient is the explainability layer to adversarial manipulation or
explanation-poisoning attacks aimed at misleading security analysts?

3. METHODOLOGY & FRAMEWORK COMPONENTS
The XAI-IDR methodology spans four primary benchmark datasets: NSL-KDD, CIC-IDS2017, CIC-IDS2018,
and IoT Botnet datasets. These cover diverse attack types (including DDoS, brute force, botnet traffic, and
scanning reconnaissance) under highly imbalanced, realistic traffic distributions. Preprocessing includes feature
normalization, redundancy removal, and synthetic class balancing to offset majority-class bias.
Three Coupled Framework Pillars
The framework is composed of three interconnected modules designed to transition network traffic from
detection to explained response:
l
Hybrid Detection Engine: Fuses spatial/temporal deep models (1D CNN + LSTM) with tree-based learners
(XGBoost + Random Forest). The deep branch captures complex sequential traffic patterns, while the tree branch
learns structured, tabular feature interactions and contributes a naturally interpretable signal to the overall
ensemble score.
l
Explanation Modules (XAI): Generates both instance-level explanations ('why was this specific flow flagged?')
and global explanations ('which features are driving the model's overall decisions?') utilizing model-agnostic SHAP
and LIME, alongside attention weights from the deep branch.
l
Policy-Driven Response Orchestrator: Applies an automated gating logic. Actions are triggered automatically
only when prediction confidence and explanation stability clear predefined thresholds. If either fails to meet the
criteria, the alert is immediately escalated to a human SOC analyst.

output either automated mitigation actions or human-in-the-loop escalations, keeping a complete audit log of
both the action and the explanation payload.

5. GATED DECISION ORCHESTRATION
A key contribution of the paper is the confidence-gated response logic. Traditional IDSs suffer from binary
decision-making where a high-threat prediction immediately triggers mitigation, resulting in costly false
containment. XAI-IDR introduces a secondary safety gate based on explanation stability. When a detection
occurs, the model's prediction confidence is evaluated against threshold tn. If passed, the system computes
local SHAP feature attributions and evaluates their mathematical stability under minor input perturbations
against threshold tn. An automated action is only taken if both thresholds are cleared. Otherwise, the threat is
escalated to the SOC analyst with the pre-computed feature attributions appended to the ticket, drastically
reducing human triage time.

7. CORE STRENGTHS & VALUE PROPOSITION
l
Operational Focus: Targets a critical operational gap in SOC workflow trust, prioritizing safe, automated
mitigation over simply achieving incremental improvements in standalone classification accuracy.
l
Hybrid Architectural Synergy: Deep learning networks provide raw detection capabilities, while tree-based
classifiers stabilize overall feature attributions and enable robust, human-aligned interpretations.
l
Dual Gated Gating Pattern: Introducing 'explanation stability' as a secondary safety gate is a highly novel,
literature-backed approach to mitigating the risk of false-positive automated quarantines.
l
Robust Threat Modeling: Unlike many XAI-IDS publications, the paper explicitly accounts for
explanation-poisoning and feature-manipulation attacks targeted directly at misleading explainability pipelines.
8. CRITICAL WEAKNESSES & RESEARCH GAPS
l
Unquantified Real-Time Latency: The claim of 'lightweight, near real-time explanation generation' is stated but
lacks concrete latency benchmarking (e.g., CPU/GPU costs, milliseconds per flow) to verify scalable performance.
l
Qualitative Robustness Claims: Robustness to adversarial perturbation and XAI-pipeline threats is discussed
narratively, but is not validated experimentally via named adversarial attacks or quantified attack success rates.
l
Lack of ATT&CK Mapping: Explanations are restricted to mathematical, feature-level attributions (e.g., flow
duration, packet rate). They are not mapped to a standardized taxonomy (e.g., MITRE ATT&CK), limiting direct
SOC actionability.
l
Static Training Protocol: The authors do not address model retraining, concept drift, or continual learning as
network traffic and threat distributions evolve over time.
9. RELEVANCE & INTEGRATION TO OUR SYSTEM
For our Final Year Project (FYP) and the design of Pillar C (Trust & Risk), this paper serves as direct,
peer-reviewed validation. We adopt several concepts while actively addressing the gaps identified in the
authors' research:
l
Validation of SHAP Layer: Validates our architectural selection of SHAP for producing stable, domain-aligned
feature attributions across multiple benchmark IDS datasets (Objective 4.4 / Section 7.3 design).
l
Closing Gaps with MITRE ATT&CK Mapping: While XAI-IDR's explanations stop at feature attribution (e.g.,
'high packet count'), our system actively maps these feature attributions to standardized MITRE ATT&CK
techniques. This provides SOC analysts with clear, actionable threat context instead of raw statistical indices.
l
Benchmarking Explanation Latency: To address the paper's main gap, our project explicitly measures and
reports explanation-generation latency, mitigating overhead via TreeSHAP and an asynchronous explain queue.
l
Red-Team Explanation Hardening: We extend our red-team testing harness (Objective 4.5) to include
explanation-integrity attacks, directly demonstrating and testing the XAI-pipeline vulnerability that the paper only
names conceptually.
l
Adoption of MTTC: We incorporate Mean Time to Containment (MTTC) as a core evaluation metric alongside
triage time to quantify operational improvements in a live, multi-pillar testbed environment.
Synthesis & Action Plan: XAI-IDR provides the foundational confidence-gating pattern for automated threat
containment. Our project directly builds upon this work by expanding explanations from mathematical feature
scores to human-actionable MITRE ATT&CK threat techniques, while explicitly quantifying XAI latency and
red-teaming explanation-poisoning threats.

Title Problem-Space Structural Adversarial Attacks for Network

Authors Andrea Venturi, Dario Stabili, Mirco Marchetti

Problem the paper addresses: GNN-based Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) are
increasingly popular because they use both flow features and network topology to detect attacks.
Prior adversarial-ML research only tested these models against feature-level perturbations (changing
packet size, duration, etc.). No one had tested whether attackers could evade GNN-NIDS by
manipulating the graph structure itself — i.e., the actual property that makes GNNs different from
traditional classifiers. This paper closes that gap.
2. Objective / Research Problem
The researchers set out to answer:
1. Can adversarial attacks be designed that specifically target the graph structure (not just
features) that GNN-based NIDS rely on?
2. What real-world constraints limit an attacker who wants to alter a network graph without direct
access to the model or the graph-generation pipeline?
3. How vulnerable are state-of-the-art GNN-NIDS (E-GraphSAGE and LineGraphSAGE) to these
structural attacks compared to their known resilience against feature attacks?
In short: “GNN-NIDS resist feature attacks — but are they equally resistant to structural ones?”
3. Methodology / Approach
Datasets used: - CTU-13 — real botnet traffic (5 malware families: Neris, Rbot, Virut, Menti, Murlo) -
ToN-IoT — IoT network traffic with 8 attack types (Backdoor, DDoS, DoS, Injection, Password,
Ransomware, Scanning, XSS)
Preprocessing: - Standard netflow preprocessing pipeline (per prior GNN-NIDS literature) - 80:20
train/test split - Malicious : benign ratio fixed at 1:10 per set (to mimic real-world class imbalance) - One
binary classifier trained per attack type (per dataset), i.e., 13 classifiers total\nExperimental setup: - Two graph representations tested: flow graph (edges = netflows) and line
graph (nodes = netflows, via a linearization procedure) - Gray-box threat model: attacker knows a
GNN-NIDS is deployed but has no access to the flow exporter, graph generator, or model internals;
attacker has already compromised ≥1 host
Proposed methodology — problem-space constraints: The authors formalize four constraints any
realistic structural attack must satisfy: 1. Available transformations — only addition operations (new
edges/nodes) are feasible; deletions require access to protected infrastructure 2. Preserved
semantics — the original malicious traffic’s functionality must not break 3. Plausibility — injected
netflows must look like authentic traffic 4. Robustness to preprocessing — must survive defenses
like anti-spoofing filters
4. Models / Architecture
Detectors evaluated: - E-GraphSAGE — GraphSAGE applied to a flow graph (nodes = IP:port pairs,
edges = netflows); performs edge classification - LineGraphSAGE — GraphSAGE applied to a line
graph derived from the flow graph via linearization (each netflow becomes a node); performs node
classification - Baseline comparison: Random Forest (RF) — traditional ML classifier, used to
benchmark feature-attack severity

10. Scope
In Scope (per the paper): - Linux/general network telemetry — netflow-based graphs only -

fused/ensembled detectors — only standalone binary GNN-NIDS evaluated
Summary
This paper is the first to formalize and test structural (graph-topology) adversarial attacks against
GNN-based NIDS, in contrast to the feature-level attacks the field had focused on until now. Using a
realistic, addition-only threat model, the authors show that while E-GraphSAGE and LineGraphSAGE are
highly robust to feature perturbations (unlike traditional Random Forest classifiers), they are severely
vulnerable to structural perturbations — in the worst case, injecting a single extra benign netflow
from a compromised host is enough to collapse detection rate on several botnet families.

graph/edge-classification), but neither is immune at scale. The paper leaves defenses, fused-detector

1. Paper Overview & Core Contribution
Deep learning models for Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS), such as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), achieve
high detection accuracy but suffer from extreme black-box opacity. While Explainable AI (XAI) is widely studied
in computer vision and healthcare, its applicability to network security and host system telemetry is
under-explored. This paper applies two prominent post-hoc, model-agnostic XAI methods—LIME (Local
Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)—to a black-box IoT
intrusion detection MLP. The authors take a crucial step further by experimentally validating the explanations
through a feature perturbation protocol and conducting a 24-person human survey to evaluate user trust and
usability.
Core Discovery: While generating local explanations is easy, verifying their correctness is harder. The paper
shows that LIME and SHAP generate broadly similar aggregate feature attributions but diverge on specific
instances, indicating complex non-linear feature interactions. In user surveys, LIME was preferred for
user-friendly visuals, while technical experts leaned toward SHAP for its theoretical consistency.

them. This paper addresses this gap by exploring:

3. Methodology & Approach
Dataset: Utilizes a generated version of the ADFA-LD dataset consisting of system call sequences. Sequences
of 30 system calls (drawn from 149 distinct syscall types) are labeled with attack patterns (Password Brute
Force, Denial of Service, Network Scan). The finalized dataset contains 52,656 instances.
Feature Engineering: Processes system call logs via 2-gram vectorization, generating 2,805 distinct n-gram
features. Dimensionality reduction is applied using a Chi-squared selector, yielding a final fixed 150-feature
vector per instance weighted using a TF-IDF-style representation.
Post-hoc XAI Methods: Applies LimeTabularExplainer and KernelExplainer (SHAP) to the black-box MLP,
extracting the top-10 most influential features/syscalls per flow.
Validation Perturbation Protocol: Tests explanation correctness via three batches:
1. Remove Top-10: Eliminate the top-10 explained system calls from each sequence and re-evaluate.
2. Class-Exclusive Replacement: Replace the top-10 explained syscalls with syscalls exclusive to the
opposite outcome class.
3. Random Replacement: Replace the top-10 explained syscalls with random system calls as a control
baseline.
4. Models & Architecture Verified
Gaspar et al.'s Model: A supervised 4-layer feed-forward Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Classifier. It consists
of one input layer (150 features) and three dense layers: Dense 1 (128 units, ReLU), Dense 2 (64 units, ReLU),
and Dense 3 (1 unit, Sigmoid) yielding binary anomaly labels. Implemented in TensorFlow/Keras.
Our Host Pillar (P3): An unsupervised, symmetric Reconstruction Autoencoder. It takes a host FeatureVector
(eBPF syscall sequences, PIDs, timings) and passes it through an encoder bottleneck (64fi32fi16fi8) and a
symmetrical decoder (8fi16fi32fi64fiN). It trains only on normal traffic and flags anomalies based on
reconstruction error.

1. Paper Overview & Core Contribution
Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) achieve state-of-the-art
results on synthetic benchmarks, but they rely heavily on target encoding to pre-process categorical
features (such as HTTP/TCP protocol fields). Target encoding computes the empirical correlation between
these categories and labels, silently depending on annotated labels even in supposedly 'unsupervised'
setups. This paper addresses this label dependency bottleneck by introducing in-context self-supervised
pre-training combined with dense vector representations.
Core Discovery: By using dense vector embeddings for categorical features alongside self-supervised
pre-training (Anomal-E), the proposed model breaks target encoding's hidden label dependency. It reaches
over 98% of fully supervised state-of-the-art performance using less than 4% of labeled data on the
NF-UQ-NIDS-V2 dataset.
2. Objectives & Key Research Questions
The primary focus of the researchers was to investigate whether self-supervised pre-training could mitigate
the high annotation cost of NIDS deployment. Three specific questions are explored:
• Q1. Performance of Dense Representation: How does dense representation perform compared to target
encoding when both are evaluated without pre-training?
• Q2. Value of Pre-training: Does in-context self-supervised pre-training consistently enhance GNN
classification performance?
• Q3. Labeled Data Efficiency: Can in-context pre-training yield high-performing intrusion detection even
when labeled data is scarce?\nREFERENCE PAPER REVIEW • GROUP 8 • BCS753 GNN NIDS BENCHMARKING
3. Methodology & Approach
Datasets & Sub-sampling: Evaluated on ToN-IoT (461,043 records, 40 features, 31 categorical) and
NF-UQ-NIDS-V2 (75,987,976 records, 43 features, 15 categorical; compiled from UNSW-NB15, ToN-IoT,
CSE-CIC-IDS2018, and BoT-IoT). Training sets are sub-sampled and class-balanced to 130,000 training
and 50,000 testing flow records.
Graph Construction: Constructed undirected multi-graphs where hosts represent nodes and flows
represent edges. Numeric and categorical edge features are concatenated. Node features are initialized as
constant vectors of 1's (length 64), meaning the topology and edge characteristics carry the signal, rather
than node identity. Source IPs are synthetically generated to anonymize topology.
Feature Representation: Numeric features undergo learned linear projection. Categorical features are
mapped via one-hot encoding followed by linear projection (proved mathematically equivalent to a learned
embedding lookup). These embeddings are merged into a final edge/node representation.
Self-Supervised Pre-training: Implements Anomal-E, a contrastive Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)
technique based on Deep Graph Infomax (DGI). It maximizes the mutual information between local edge
embeddings and a global graph summary, contrasting the real graph against a corrupted graph generated by
randomly shuffling edge features.
4. Models & Architecture Verified
Gu et al.'s Model: Consists of an E-GraphSAGE encoder (2 layers, hidden dimension 128, edge
representation 256) and an Anomal-E discriminator during pre-training. For detection, the pre-trained
encoder is fine-tuned by appending a supervised classification head.
Our Network Pillar (P1): A dual-path model running a baseline reconstruction Autoencoder (for
zero-label anomaly detection) in parallel with a GNN-temporal model (GNN + LSTM over host-to-host graph
snapshots) to capture relational and sequence patterns. The sub-scores are then fused via a learned
ensembler.

1. Paper Overview & Core Contribution
Modern network security heavily relies on deep learning architectures like autoencoders to identify complex
anomalies and zero-day attacks. However, these complex models operate as opaque 'black boxes', generating
outputs whose underlying logic is entirely unintelligible to human operators (Sovrano & Vitali, 2024). To bridge this
gap, Padín-Torrente et al. (2026) introduce a novel, Human-Centered Explainable AI (HC-XAI) pipeline designed
to translate technical anomaly scores and SHAP-based feature attributions into intuitive, context-aware Natural
Language Explanations (NLEs) tailored specifically for Security Operations Center (SOC) analysts.
The core contribution of this work lies in its integrated, end-to-end architecture that combines unsupervised network
anomaly detection, local feature attribution, and natural language synthesis. By leveraging lightweight,
locally-hosted Large Language Models (LLMs) loaded with 4-bit double quantization, the pipeline generates
high-quality, actionable alerts without the privacy, regulatory, and financial constraints of cloud-based APIs. Crucially,
the authors integrate a Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) feedback loop powered by Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG). When a security analyst refines an alert explanation, the updated pair is saved back into the database,
dynamically grounding future explanations in validated human expertise and allowing the system to adapt to
emerging threats and zero-day attacks without model retraining.
2. Objectives & Research Problem
Traditional explainable AI (XAI) frameworks in cybersecurity stop at visual or saliency-based feature attributions (such
as bar charts of SHAP or LIME values). While technically accurate, these outputs are highly abstract, adding
cognitive load and leading to frequent misunderstandings during high-stress incident responses (Szymanski et al.,

(cid:127) Objective I - Unsupervised Network-Level Detection: Train a deep autoencoder solely on benign traffic from

reconstruction error.
(cid:127) Objective II - Efficient Local Feature Attribution: Adapt KernelSHAP to approximate feature-level

(cid:127) Objective III - Grounded Natural Language Explanations: Develop a local, quantized LLM generation

The proposed HC-XAI workflow operates in five distinct phases. (1) Data Analysis & Preprocessing: Uses the
CIC-IDS-2018 dataset, representing realistic enterprise network traffic with over 13 million benign flows and 14
distinct attack types (Sharafaldin et al., 2018). Raw data is cleaned, multi-day captures are merged (excluding the
inconsistent Feb 20 capture), and normalized using a two-step approach: Robust Scaling to minimize outlier
influence, followed by Min-Max Scaling to aid optimization. (2) Anomaly Detection: An unsupervised deep
autoencoder is trained on benign-only data (60% training, 10% validation). Anomaly threshold is established as the
95th percentile of validation errors. (3) SHAP Integration: For any anomaly, the top N features with the highest
reconstruction error are isolated. KernelSHAP is applied to explain the reconstructed values of these features using
the K-means-summarized background centroids. (4) Human-Centered Explanations: Local LLMs combine technical
SHAP values with RAG context. (5) NLE Alignment & HITL Feedback: Security analysts review LLM alerts,
contributing expert-level corrections directly back into the RAG database to ground subsequent alerts in validated
knowledge.
4. Models & Architecture Verified
The unsupervised anomaly detection architecture consists of a symmetric deep autoencoder neural network with 6
hidden layers in the encoder and 6 hidden layers in the decoder (plus a central latent space layer).
Hyperparameters derived from extensive tuning include: 100 epochs with early stopping on MSE loss, learning rate of
0.01, batch size of 256, dropout rate of 0.01, L2 regularization of 0.001, and ReLU activation function (except the final
reconstruction layers). For explanation synthesis, the authors evaluate five instruction-tuned local LLMs under 4-bit
double quantization to minimize local VRAM footprint: Qwen1.5-4B-Chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9b-it, and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct.

This benchmark paper presents a rigorous, systematic review of deep learning-based User Behavior Analytics (UBA)
and User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) models for proactive cyber threat detection [19]. The authors analyze
how context-aware deep learning architectures—specifically Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) networks, Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), and
Transformer-based models—can be integrated to mitigate insider threats [19, 31]. The paper highlights the paradigm
shift from static, reactive security policies to proactive risk management driven by personalized risk profiling, user
micro-segmentation, and dynamic, automated interventions aligned with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 standards [19, 28, 29].

3. METHODOLOGY &AMP; SEARCH STRATEGY (PRISMA PROCESS)
To ensure high scientific rigor, reproducibility, and transparent reporting, the authors executed this systematic review
in strict compliance with the **PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses)**
guidelines [137]. A multi-database search was conducted across core repositories: IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ACM
Digital Library, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Scopus, and arXiv.org [138]. The search queries restricted literature
published between 2010 and 2025 (later updated through mid-2026 to incorporate cutting-edge publications) [139,
140].
The search syntax combined deep learning descriptors and domain-specific terms using explicit Boolean operators,
e.g., ('deep learning in cybersecurity' OR 'user and entity behavior analytics (UEBA)' OR 'LSTM for anomaly
detection' OR 'GRU for insider threat detection') [139]. Out of an initial pool of 2,660 records identified across all
sources, a stringent multi-stage screening funnel was applied, resulting in a final synthesis pool of **159 high-quality
peer-reviewed papers** [142].

4. COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS OF REVIEWED ALGORITHMS
Paper 1 provides a comprehensive taxonomic evaluation mapping several Machine Learning (ML) and Deep
Learning (DL) algorithms to behavioral anomaly detection [47]. Key findings highlight distinct structural characteristics
across model categories:
Traditional Machine Learning (KNN, SVM, RF, DBSCAN): These models are fast, simple to implement, and
computationally lightweight, making them highly effective for localized anomaly detection [52, 53, 54, 57]. However,
they rely heavily on handcrafted feature engineering, fail to capture sequential temporal relationships, and suffer
severe performance degradation when exposed to highly complex, multi-dimensional security streams [146].
Deep Learning Sequential Models (LSTM, GRU, RNN): Architectures specifically engineered to process sequential
logs by utilizing internal gating mechanisms to maintain historical cell states and resolve the vanishing gradient
problem [19, 103]. While highly effective at modeling temporal user baselines, they are computationally intensive and
sensitive to hyperparameter initialization [64, 65].

Attention Mechanisms & Transformers: Self-attention processes allow the network to dynamically assign weights
to critical high-risk behavioral features (e.g., unusual login times combined with massive exfiltration volumes) across
massive time scales [104, 106]. The parallelizability of Transformers allows scaling, though training requires
substantial data and high computational power [106].
Generative & Hybrid Models (VAE, GAN, LSTM-RF): Generative models like Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
learn to reconstruct normal behavior baselines and flag anomalies via reconstruction error [69]. Hybrid structures
(such as combining an LSTM's sequential features with a Random Forest classifier) attempt to unify sequential
tracking with robust, transparent decision-making [108].

6. RELEVANCE, CONTRADICTIONS &AMP; GAPS
The systematic findings of Paper 1 provide a powerful scientific precedent for the architectural design choices of our
**Identity UEBA Pillar (P2)**, while identifying critical gaps that we actively address:
1. Resolving the Epistemic Blindness of Deterministic DL: The authors of Paper 1 make a critical theoretical
observation: while attention mechanisms highlight important features, they remain deterministic and 'epistically
blind'—meaning they cannot express how certain a model is about its classification [200, 202]. To resolve this, our
**P2 Engine implements Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout as an Epistemic Uncertainty Estimator**. By executing random
dropout masks during live inference, we construct a probability distribution over the anomaly score, allowing us to
Reviewed Paper: Akampurira et al. (F1000Research, 2026) Page 5\nFINAL YEAR PROJECT • LITERATURE REVIEW BENCHMARK REPORT GROUP 8 • SUBJECT CODE: BCS753
publish both an anomaly index and a confidence boundary [207]. This directly addresses a major gap highlighted in
Paper 1.
2. Strict Alignment with ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and NIST: Paper 1 notes that DL-driven UEBA literature rarely details
concrete mapping to compliance controls [260]. In our P2 Pillar, we map user baselines directly to specific revised
controls: **Control A.5.7 (Threat Intelligence)** via live feed of suspicious behaviors, **Control A.8.16 (Monitoring
Activities)** by establishing continuous session verification, and **Control A.8.2 (Privileged Access Rights)** by
immediately escalating the risk scoring of administrative accounts demonstrating temporal baseline anomalies.
3. Context-Aware Multi-Head Attention: Insiders easily blend in with normal activities unless evaluated within
context [125]. P2 implements a **Multi-Head Self-Attention Layer** that contextually correlates active directory logs,
geolocation anomalies, authentication timings, and resource transfer volumes [162]. This prevents uniform
thresholding and reduces false positives on legitimate but rare actions.
4. Low-Latency Automated Interventions: While the literature focuses on retrospective analysis, our P2 design
triggers live **automated micro-segmentation** and **user isolation** (leveraging SOAR integrations) immediately
when a high-risk transaction exceeds confidence thresholds [181, 183].

8. PEER REVIEW REFLECTIONS &AMP; ACADEMIC RIGOR
A key element of Paper 1's scholarly context is its public peer-review status on the F1000Research gateway [18,
142]. The manuscript achieved **'Approved with Reservations'** from two prominent international experts. In
compiling this benchmark report, Group 8 has integrated the core critical feedback of these reviewers into our
architectural positioning, ensuring a standard of academic rigor that addresses their technical reservations:
• Reviewer 1 (Abayomi Titilola Olutimehin, Royal Holloway University of London): Highlighted that many
reviewed models overstate their operational readiness, relying heavily on static benchmark datasets (such as CERT
and KDD99) without discussing the risks of synthetic dataset dependence [256, 258]. In our P2 Pillar, we address this
critical limitation by evaluating our architecture on live, heterogeneous organizational Active Directory telemetry and
deploying active ADWIN monitors to capture real-world data drift in real-time [167].
• Reviewer 2 (Hewa Majeed Zangana, Duhok Polytechnic University): Criticized the lack of standby reproducibility
in the paper's methodology, noting that search parameters and Boolean strings were omitted in the main text [252].
Furthermore, Zangana requested a concrete mapping of UEBA capabilities to specific ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A
controls [253]. We have resolved this by providing explicit, standalone search strings in Section 3 and structurally
engineering direct mappings to Controls A.5.7, A.8.16, and A.8.2 in our architectural comparative matrix [253].
